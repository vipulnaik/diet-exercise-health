# Reduced support standing exercises (with history)

This is a unifying document covering a wide range of reduced support
standing exercises, with outward links to individual exercises for
more details.

Reduced support standing exercises are standing exercises that involve
less support than both legs flat on the ground, and also require
staying within the given suppot pattern for the duration of the
exercise. The proximal goal during the exercise is to maximize the
duration.

Many of these exercises are scheduled as part of the [balance exercise
cycle](balance-exercise-cycle-with-history.md).

## Goals and functional relevance of exercises

To a first approximation, reduced support standing exercises help
improve both balance and strength.

### Balance

Balance is improved because reduced support configurations are less
stable, because we have to make sure that the center of mass of the
body is within the convex hull of the contact area with the ground,
which is relatively reduced, and that the contact forces are managed
in a way as to keep this so.

Therefore, more balancing skill is required to sustain them. This
shows up in several ways:

* Predictive balance: This is skill at determining an initial
  configuration that is relatively stable.

* Reactive balance: This is skill at determining and responding to
  fluctuations in my configuration over time and reacting to them via
  compensatory movements and muscle activations, e.g., putting more
  weight on one side.

### Strength and endurance

Strength is improved because reduced support configurations channel
the body weight over a smaller support area (maybe one leg instead of
two, or just the toes or heels instead of the whole foot).

The specific muscles involved depend on what part of the foot is in
contact with the ground.

### The interplay between balance and strength

Balance and strength are hard to separate out. That's because better
balance reduces the strength requirement somewhat. With better
balance, initial configurations chose are more stable, so they need
less strength to preserve, and any deviations from the configuration
are handled more quickly, so less strength is needed. Conversely, more
strength can somewhat compensate for poor balance; even without fast
reactive catches to loss of balance, having more strength allows for a
later, less efficient catch.

## Terminological foundation for understanding the different variations

### Support pattern

The support pattern describes what parts of my foot I am using for
support. Typical support patterns include:

* Toes (this means both the toes and the base of the toes (there's
  another pattern of standing on balls of toes, which I do not
  include)

* Full foot (flat)

* Heel (without toe support)

* Heel (with toe support)

* Medial side of foot

* Lateral side of foot

### Legs

The following are some typical patterns:

* Bilateral symmetric: Both legs are using the same support pattern.

* Unilateral: I'm standing on just one leg using the specified support
  pattern, with the other leg in the air (usually golded).

* Bilateral asymmetric (not yet explored): I'm using a different
  support pattern for each leg.

### Eyes (open versus closed)

Whether my eyes are open or closed is another parameter in the
definition of a balance exercise.

A small protocol clarification. For eyes-open exercise, the exercise
starts as soon as I either arrange both legs in the desired support
pattern, or arrange one leg and then lift the other leg off. On the
other hand, for eyes-closed exercises, I generally take a few seconds
to achieve the support pattern and liftoff, stabilize, then generally
starting when the second counter is a multiple of 5, close my
eyes. The duration is measured starting when I close my eyes.

For every exercise, the eyes-closed version is harder than the
eyes-open version, primarily because an important sensory input that
influences balance is removed. The hardness of eyes-closed operates
most directly via balance, but it also affects strength indirectly
because of the interplay between balance and strength. Therefore:

* Eyes-closed exercises tend to be more time-efficient than eyes-open
  exercises in terms of training and testing balance and strength,
  once we cross some baseline stability threshold.

* Relatively speaking, the eyes-open exercise is better *relatively*
  at training strength relative to balance. Therefore, if
  time-efficiency is not a constraint and strength is a higher
  priority than balance (or balance is unresponsive to training and is
  bottlenecking the ability to keep pushing strength further), the
  eyes-open exercise may be better.

* The ratio of the eyes-closed duration and the eyes-open duration is
  a good proxy for the relative roles of balance and strength for the
  exercise.

### Shoes

The default execution pattern for the exercises is at home, wearing
socks but no shoes. For any exercise, a "with shoes" variant can be considered.

The "with shoes" variant has the functional benefit that it more
accurately measures and trains balance and strength in the outdoor
context, where I am wearing shoes. This is particularly important for
balance, where the specific skills being trained might be a little
different with shoes on, since the feedback from the ground contact is
transmitted differently through shoes.

Against this, it has two disadvantages:

* Since I don't wear shoes at home, this is limited to cases when I
  am outdoors, and I generally have less discretionary outdoor time to
  fit in exercises of variable length, since I can't multiplex with
  other things I need to do and can't multiplex it with listening to
  stuff.

* Particularly the eyes-closed exercises are sufficiently hard that
  they don't cross the stability threshold where I can use them for
  training. The eyes-open exercises are better.

* Outdoor environments vary in their evenness and other
  characteristics, so differences in performance can be partly a
  result of such variation. For instance, the waiting area for traffic
  crossings tends to be a little slanted rather than flat, and this
  reduces stability significantly. In contrast, indoor spaces like the
  laundry room tend to be flatter and allow for longer durations.

## Working through the sequence of events in a given exercise execution

This section is intended to give a general sense of what happens when
I am executing a given reduced support standing exercise.

### Setup: identification of stable configuration

I begin by setting up a configuration matching the support pattern
that seems reasonably stable. In the eyes-open case, counting starts
as soon as I achieve the support pattern (and liftoff of the other leg
if it is a unilateral exercise). In the eyes-closed case, I take a few
seconds after achieving the support pattern and liftoff before closing
my eyes and starting the counting.
