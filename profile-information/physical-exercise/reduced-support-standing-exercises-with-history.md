# Reduced support standing exercises (with history)

This is a unifying document covering a wide range of reduced support
standing exercises, with outward links to individual exercises for
more details.

Reduced support standing exercises are standing exercises that involve
less support than both legs flat on the ground, and also require
staying within the given support pattern for the duration of the
exercise. The proximal goal during the exercise is to maximize the
duration.

Many of these exercises are scheduled as part of the [balance exercise
cycle](balance-exercise-cycle-with-history.md). However, not every
exercise in the balance exercise cycle is a reduced support standing
exercise, and not every reduced support standing exercise is in the
balance exercise cycle.

jargon that is best understood by reading the document up to the
point, but can be approximately understood even without that).

## Goals and functional relevance of exercises

To a first approximation, reduced support standing exercises help
improve both balance and strength.

### Balance

Balance is improved because reduced support configurations are less
stable, because we have to make sure that the center of mass of the
body is within the convex hull of the contact area with the ground,
which is relatively reduced, and that the contact forces are managed
in a way as to keep this so.

Therefore, more balancing skill is required to sustain them. This
shows up in several ways:

* Predictive balance: This is skill at determining an initial
  configuration that is relatively stable.

* Reactive balance: This is skill at determining and responding to
  fluctuations in my configuration over time and reacting to them via
  compensatory movements and muscle activations, e.g., putting more
  weight on one side.

### Strength and endurance

Strength is improved because reduced support configurations channel
the body weight over a smaller support area (maybe one leg instead of
two, or just the toes or heels instead of the whole foot).

Strength is also improved as a result of the specific bursts of
strength needed when reacting to a loss of balance. The amount of
strength needed depends on the extent of balance loss and how I react
to it. This is bursty, brief use of high strength, whereas maintenance
of stable configuration is about enduring for long duration at a
somewhat lower strength level.

The specific leg muscles involved depend on what part of the foot is
in contact with the ground (the muscles are not just the muscles at
the point of contact, but muscles of the heel and leg, but there's a
mapping between what part of the foot is in contact and what leg
muscles are most involved).

### The interplay between balance and strength

Balance and strength are hard to separate out. That's because better
balance reduces the strength requirement somewhat. With better
balance, initial configurations chose are more stable, so they need
less strength to preserve, and any deviations from the configuration
are handled more quickly, so less strength is needed. Conversely, more
strength can somewhat compensate for poor balance; even without fast
reactive catches to loss of balance, having more strength allows for a
later, less efficient catch.

## Terminological foundation for understanding the different variations

### Support pattern

The support pattern describes what parts of my foot I am using for
support. Typical support patterns include:

* Toes (this means both the toes and the base of the toes (there's
  another pattern of standing on balls of toes, which I do not
  include)

* Full foot (flat)

* Heel (without toe support)

* Heel (with toe support)

The following are possibilities that have not been explored yet:

* Medial side of foot

* Lateral side of foot

### Legs

The following are some typical patterns:

* Bilateral symmetric: Both legs are using the same support pattern.

* Unilateral: I'm standing on just one leg using the specified support
  pattern, with the other leg in the air (usually folded with the knee
  anterior to me of me and the foot within my coronal plane).

* Bilateral asymmetric (not yet explored): I'm using a different
  support pattern for each leg.

### Eyes (open versus closed)

Whether my eyes are open or closed is another parameter in the
definition of a balance exercise.

A small protocol clarification. For eyes-open exercise, the exercise
starts as soon as I either arrange both legs in the desired support
pattern, or arrange one leg and then lift the other leg off. On the
other hand, for eyes-closed exercises, I generally take a few seconds
to achieve the support pattern and liftoff, stabilize, then generally
starting when the second counter is a multiple of 5, close my
eyes. The duration is measured starting when I close my eyes.

For every exercise, the eyes-closed version is harder than the
eyes-open version, primarily because an important sensory input that
influences balance is removed. The hardness of eyes-closed operates
most directly via balance, but it also affects strength indirectly
because of the interplay between balance and strength. Therefore:

* Eyes-closed exercises tend to be more time-efficient than eyes-open
  exercises in terms of training and testing balance and strength,
  once we cross some baseline stability threshold.

* Relatively speaking, the eyes-open exercise is better *relatively*
  at training strength relative to balance. Therefore, if
  time-efficiency is not a constraint and strength is a higher
  priority than balance (or balance is unresponsive to training and is
  bottlenecking the ability to keep pushing strength further), the
  eyes-open exercise may be better.

* The ratio of the eyes-closed duration and the eyes-open duration is
  a good proxy for the relative roles of balance and strength for the
  exercise.

### Shoes

The default execution pattern for the exercises is at home, wearing
socks but no shoes. For any exercise, a "with shoes" variant can be considered.

The "with shoes" variant has the functional benefit that it more
accurately measures and trains balance and strength in the outdoor
context, where I am wearing shoes. This is particularly important for
balance, where the specific skills being trained might be a little
different with shoes on, since the feedback from the ground contact is
transmitted differently through shoes.

Against this, it has two disadvantages:

* Since I don't wear shoes at home, this is limited to cases when I
  am outdoors, and I generally have less discretionary outdoor time to
  fit in exercises of variable length, since I can't multiplex with
  other things I need to do and can't multiplex it with listening to
  stuff.

* Particularly the eyes-closed exercises are sufficiently hard that
  they don't cross the stability threshold where I can use them for
  training. The eyes-open exercises are better.

* Outdoor environments vary in their evenness and other
  characteristics, so differences in performance can be partly a
  result of such variation. For instance, the waiting area for traffic
  crossings tends to be a little slanted rather than flat, and this
  reduces stability significantly. In contrast, indoor spaces like the
  laundry room tend to be flatter and allow for longer durations.

## Working through the sequence of events in a given exercise execution

This section is intended to give a general sense of what happens when
I am executing a given reduced support standing exercise.

### Setup: identification of stable configuration

I begin by setting up a configuration matching the support pattern
that seems reasonably stable. In the eyes-open case, counting starts
as soon as I achieve the support pattern (and liftoff of the other leg
if it is a unilateral exercise). In the eyes-closed case, I take a few
seconds after achieving the support pattern and liftoff before closing
my eyes and starting the counting.

### Maintenance of stable configuration (honeymoon period)

The honeymoon period is the initial period of a reduced support
standing exercise, while I'm still within a stable configuration with
no major challenge that causes me to almost lose balance.

Exercises can roughly be divided into three categories (it's a
multi-dimensional spectrum, but it's helpful to think of four
categories):

* (80%+ honeymoon period) Exercises where it's easy to start off with
  a configuration that's highly stable, so that the honeymoon period
  is maintained almost all the way to the end of the exercise, and the
  thing that gives at the end is strength/endurance rather than
  unexpected loss of balance. For instance, standing on toes (both
  legs) is more like this than standing on one leg (flat). Eyes-open
  exercises tend to be more like this than their eyes-closed
  counterparts. Roughly speaking 80%+ of the exercise duration is
  spent in the honeymoon period, and sudden loss of balance before
  fatigue has already started setting in is rare.

* (30-70% honeymoon period) Exercises where most configurations are at
  least moderately stable, but there is a wide range of barely stable
  configurations, and it is possible to randomly lose balance even
  well before reaching the point of fatigue. After loss of balance,
  the new configuration is on average likely to be less stable than
  the original one, which is compounded by fatigue and makes it
  effectively even less stable. For such exercises, the honeymoon
  period is generally between 30% and 70% of the total exercise
  duration, and can occasionally go even lower or even higher. An
  example is standing on one leg (flat) with eyes closed.

* (10-40% honeymoon period) Exercises where there are no moderately
  stable configurations; all configurations are unstable to moderately
  sized perturbations, but it is possible to "catch" oneself in many
  cases. An example is standing on one leg toes with eyes open. The
  honeymoon period tends to be relatively brief.

* (concept of honeymoon period makes no sense) Exercises where even
  the mildest perturbation to a configuration leads to loss of
  balance. An example is standing on heels with eyes open. In some
  sense, the entire duration is the honeymoon period, but in another
  sense, there's no honeymoon period given the impending doom.

### Support pattern maintenance after the end of the honeymoon period

The honeymoon period ends with a loss of balance of some sort that
gets caught quickly enough that the support pattern is
maintained. However, two things happen:

* Probabilistically, the new configuration is likely to be less stable
  than the original one, since it was a reflexive recovery rather than
  a carefully chosen one. It may or may not be possible to transition
  to a more stable configuration.

* The act of rapidly catching the loss of balance is more fatiguing
  than the maintenance, so there's a marked increase in fatigue over
  and above the gradual accumulation fatigue during the honeymoon
  period.

Both of these make the time after the honeymoon period more
challenging and taxing for balance maintenance and more demanding of
strength an endurance. This can lead to further losses of balance,
that may or may not get caught. Each such loss of balance leads to a
repeat of the two effects described above.

## Other related exercises outside the scope of this document

### Non-stationary exercises

There are several related exercises that build on one of the support
patterns described here and train a mix of balance and
strength/endurance, but fall outside the scope of this document. A few
categories:

* Exercises involving back-and-forth between support patterns without
  the whole body moving forward. These may be measured in either
  repetitions or total duration or a mix. An example is repeated toe
  raises followed by letdowns. The duration for which either pattern
  is maintained is brief.

* Exercises involving forward movement with a support pattern, such as
  hopping (same full foot support pattern as standing-on-one-leg, but
  now in service of forward movement) or tiptoe-walking (same toes
  support pattern as standing on toes, but now in service of forward
  movement). These can be thought of as walking-like exercises
  (support pattern for at least one leg is active at all times, with
  transitions involving both legs supported using the support pattern)
  and running-like exercises (support pattern for at most one leg is
  active at all times, with transitions involving both legs in the
  air). These may be measured in repetitions or distance covered or a
  mix.

* Exercises involving doing something while in a support pattern, such
  as tying shoelaces with one leg on the ground and the other leg (on
  which shoelaces are being tied) raised.

All the above kinds of exercises differ from our reduced support
standing exercises in that their goal is to accomplish a particular
movement. Our reduced support standing exercises have as their goal
the avoidance of movement, and failing such avoidance, the
counteracting of movement by compensatory movement and muscle
activation to maintain the support pattern.

### Exercises involving non-foot support

The connotation of "standing" here is that all support is via parts of
the feet. This excludes a bunch of other exercises:

* Dead hang and pull-ups, as well as plank and push-ups. These
  exercises involve having part or all of the body's weight supported
  by the hands via the arms. These are not "standing" exercises in the
  conventional sense.

* Kneeling exercises, as these involve weight being supported via the
  knees.

* Seated exercises, that use a similar foot contact pattern as one of
  the support patterns described above, but from a seated
  position. This means that only part (and a variable part) of body
  weight is supported via the feet, and there is no forced balance or
  strength requirement imposed on the foot contact pattern.

## Protocol parameters

### Threshold durations and retry logic

Some reduced support standing exercises are simply observational: I
execute them and record the duration, and my document for the exercise
gives the expected duration range. Failure to achieve a duration in
the expected range does not lead me to retry the exercise.

Other reduced support standing exercises have retry logic. I currently
follow two kinds of retry logic, with the selection dependent on the
exercise:

* Rigid threshold-based retry: I retry until I achieve a threshold or
  hit a maximum number of attempts, with the threshold needed itself
  potentially reducing with each retry, taking into account fatige. I
  do with for [one-leg
  eyes-closed](standing-on-one-leg-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md)
  and [toes
  eyes-closed](standing-on-toes-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md).

* Fuzzy budget-based retry: Try a few times, and record the top one or
  two, with no fixed number of attempts but an approximate total time
  budget, and the option of stopping at fewer attempts if I clear the
  expected duration range early on. This is a slightly more informal
  version of the retry logic. I do this for the [one-leg toes
  eyes-closed](standing-on-one-leg-toes-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md)
  exercise.

#### The two potential benefits of duration ranges and retries

Having known duration ranges that I am targeting, either just through
informal knowledge or through partial enforcement via retries, can
have two effects.

* Late stage effort: When I am past the honeymoon period in a given
  balance exercise and have had some losses of balance, the effort
  needed to maintain balance increases, and that's also the time
  period where the return per unit time is higher, because I'm
  training under fatigue with greater strength and balance
  requirements. This is the same idea as the strength exercise "train
  a few reps short of failure" except that failure here is loss of
  support pattern rather than the muscles giving way.

  What a duration range or threshold does is give me motivation to
  push through that late stage more than I otherwise would.

  With a rigid threshold-based retry, the incentive is more concrete
  and stronger: pushing through a few more seconds to get past the
  threshold will save me a retry, which could be several more seconds
  or minutes on the exercise. This effect is further compounded in
  eyes-closed exercises by my not knowing exactly how much time has
  passed, which means that I am incentivized to push until I am past
  the threshold with enough margin to account for the lack of exact
  measurement because my eyes are closed.

* Retries themselves: On the days that I fail to hit the threshold on
  the first try, I am often more fatigued or less on top of things to
  begin with, so I'm already training under slightly adverse
  conditions. Add on to that the fatigue from the first
  try. Therefore, retries tend to be under more adverse conditions,
  which often means that they are providing more training per unit
  time. However, doing the exercise with too much fatigue, or setting
  unusually high thresholds, can be a risk in and of itself. The
  choice of attempt-specific thresholds has to balance that
  consideration.

  This retry benefit only incurs for exercises where I have a retry
  policy, and it incurs much more cleanly and definitively for
  exercises with rigid threshold-based retry rather than fuzzy
  budget-based retry.

#### Principles governing retry policy by exercise

For exercises that are primarily bottlenecked on strength, and
generally last 2 minutes or more per attempt, retries don't make
sense. This is for several reasons:

* The primary reason is that failure happens through
  fatigue/exhaustion rather than loss of balance, and this
  fatigue/exhaustion carries over to the next attempt, making it
  effectively much shorter. For instance, if I typically go over 5
  minutes, but I end up just doing 4 minutes 50 seconds in the first
  attempt, a retry is very unlikely to get me all the way to 5 minutes
  or even somewhere remotely close; the retry will probably flame out
  in a minute or two. So, the retry just isn't feasible.

* A secondary reason is that the motivational role in late stage
  effort is more muted. While some willpower is needed to keep pushing
  through, it's a less nuanced "keep going". In contrast,
  balance-bottlenecked exercises seem to involve more sophisticated
  self-talk of the form "I'm willing to get in an awkward and unstable
  configuration for another 5 to 10 seconds so that I cross the
  threshold" and the specific threshold and how far I am from it (as
  judged approximately with eyes closed) can influence that self-talk.

For exercises that are bottlenecked on both strength and balance
without being heavily skewed to oen side, and generally last somewhere
between 30 seconds and 4 minutes per attempt, the rigid
threshold-based retry approach makes sense. The balance piece is the
one that's most responsive to training under fatigue, including late
stage effort and retries. At the same time, the durations are long
enough and non-random enough that rigid thresholds do make sense to
aim toward.

For balance-bottlenecked exercises with 10-40% honeymoon period
duration, and where each attempt is generally under a minute, the
fuzzy budget-based retry, with duration ranges for reference, makes
most sense. Since each attempt is short, packing several attempts
within a time budget is feasible. Also, given the inherent instability
of the exercise and the short honeymoon period, attempts can vary
widely in duration, and rigid thresholds can become impossible to
achieve on some days if the first few attempts went poorly, leading to
exhaustion without hitting the goal.

### Temperature and warmup

Temperature and warmup (prior movement and activity of muscles) can
play a role in the durations achieved with various exercises. Here are
a few general observations:

* More balance-bottlenecked exercises tend to be more sensitive to
  temperature and warmup than more strength-bottlenecked exercises.

* Generally speaking, temperatures below 20 degrees Celsius (68
  degrees Fahrenheit) are ones where I start facing a mild performance
  penalty for the more balance-bottlenecked exercises. To a first
  approximation, the penalty varies linearly with temperature, though
  the real picture is more complicated.

* Moderate warmup, such as walking or light jogging, well below the
  point of exhaustion, improves performance on exercises, with the
  improvement stronger on more balance-bottlenecked exercises.

#### Choice of when and where to do exercises (currently not influenced by temperature or warmup)

As of 2026, the vast majority of reduced support standing exercises
that I do regularly are part of the [balance exercise
cycle](balance-exercise-cycle-with-history.md) (as either cycle
exercises or occasional exercises). This is during interludes in rice
prep while the water is filtering. It's almost always without any
prior warmup, and the timing is not selected based on
temperature. Some other reduced support standing exercises that
require shoes are done opportunistically while outdoors, and these two
do not involve temperature control or any warmup beyond walking.

However, historically, early on in my execution of [one-leg
eyes-closed
exercises](standing-on-one-leg-toes-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md),
I would execute them both before and after jogging. On cold days, I
would often fail to hit thresholds before jogging but hit them
consistently after jogging, which speaks to the role of both
temperature and warmup.

#### Threshold selection (currently influenced by temperature for some exercises, no explicit warmup term)

The [one-leg eyes-closed
exercises](standing-on-one-leg-toes-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md),
which is relatively more balance-bottlenecked and hence
temperature-sensitive, has temperature-based adjustment to its
threshold. In contrast, the [toes eyes-closed
exercise](standing-on-toes-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md) does not
have temperature-based adjustment.

### Leg comparison and sequencing

For unilateral exercises as well as bilateral asymmetric exercises,
the duration ranges (and thresholds if applicable) could in principle
vary by leg. I do think there are slight qualitative differences
between the legs. However, in practice, these differences are small
compared to the randomness in attempts, and for simplicity, I have
kept all details of the protocol, duration ranges, and thresholds
symmetric between the legs.

The one exercise where the difference is most palpable is the [one-leg
eyes-open
exercise](standing-on-one-leg-with-eyes-closed-with-history.md), where
the right leg generally performs about 30 to 60 seconds better than
the left leg. This suggests that the leg difference is highest on
strength-bottlenecked exercises and much weaker (and even hard to put
a direction on) on balance-bottlenecked exercises.

The only sequencing note worth adding is that for unilateral
exercises, I do the right leg before the left leg. This is consistent
with my [hand gripper
exercises](hand-gripper-exercises-with-history.md) of doing the right
hand before the left hand.
